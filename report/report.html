Rare Tastes of Texas

A website with an AI shopping assistant for a family jelly business

AuthorNolan Hall
Live sitehttps://raretastesoftexas.com
GitHub repohttps://github.com/ndh26/Final-Project
CourseSOM AI Course, Final Project

In one sentence: Rare Tastes of Texas is a free-to-run website for my mom’s prickly pear and mesquite bean jelly business, with an AI assistant that answers customers’ questions from her own product and market information and passes anything it can’t answer to her as a ready-to-send message.

2. Motivation

My mom, Lisha Hall, runs a small business making homemade prickly pear jelly, spicy prickly pear jelly, mesquite bean jelly, and cinnamon rolls. She picks the fruit by hand on our family’s ranch, makes everything in small batches, and sells it at Goliad Market Days, a farmers’ market held on the second Saturday of every month in Goliad, Texas. She has always wanted a website: somewhere she could send customers she meets at the market, so they can see what she makes, find out when she’ll be back, and get in touch between market days.

How she handles it today. Everything happens face to face or by phone. Customers who want a jar between markets, a gift box, or a delivery have to remember her name, find her number, and call. Her business card gives her name, a one-line tagline, and her phone number. There is no place to show the flavors, the seasonal nature of the products, or the market schedule, and every question (“Do you have the spicy one this month?”, “Can you wrap it as a gift?”, “When’s the next market?”) costs her a phone call.

What that costs. The cost is mostly lost sales and time rather than money. A shopper who tastes the jelly once in Goliad and wants more three weeks later has no easy way back. Repeat questions take time she would rather spend making jelly. The obvious fix, a website builder such as Squarespace or Wix, costs about $17–$49 per month (roughly $200–$590 per year)[1], which is a real expense for a business that sells jars at a monthly market and doesn’t need an online store at all: her stock depends on the harvest, so she can’t promise inventory to a checkout page.

Why anyone should care. Many very small sellers (market vendors, home bakers, crafters) are in exactly this position: too small for a store or a paid website, but losing customers because they are hard to find and hard to ask. A low-cost site that answers the common questions honestly and routes the rest to the owner is useful well beyond one jelly business.

3. What is new here

Most of the pieces already exist, so it’s worth being specific about what this project does differently.

What this project does that those don’t, in combination:

  • $0 per month to run. The site is static files on Netlify’s free tier. The AI assistant costs about three hundredths of a cent per question (Section 5), with a hard daily spending cap.
  • Built around how she actually sells. There is no cart. Products are labeled seasonal, cinnamon rolls are marked “market only,” and the market page calculates the next second-Saturday date automatically so it never goes stale.
  • An assistant that knows only her facts and says so. Unlike a general chatbot, it answers only from the site’s own information, refuses to guess prices, hours, ingredients, or allergens, and turns every question it can’t answer into a pre-filled message for Lisha to answer personally.
  • Her brand, not a template. The design comes from her own business card and her “Heritage of the Hall Ranch” letter, and the product photos are her own jars with the backgrounds removed.

To be honest about it: nothing here is a technical first. The value is in fitting a small, safe AI feature into a no-cost site designed around one seller’s real workflow.

4. The agents

What the agent is and what it does

The site has one AI agent: the “Ask about our jellies” assistant, a chat box on the Products and Market pages. Its job is narrow on purpose:

  1. Answer a visitor’s question about the products, flavors, Goliad Market Days, gift packaging, deliveries, or how to order, using only the facts published on the site.
  2. When it can’t answer (prices, market hours, ingredients, allergens, custom orders, delivery to a specific town), say so plainly and hand the question to Lisha: it drafts a short, polite message that appears behind a “Send this question to Lisha →” button. Clicking it opens the Contact form with the topic and message filled in; the visitor adds their name and email and sends it.

It cannot take orders, payments, or personal details, cannot send anything by itself, and cannot change anything on the site.

Tools

ToolWhat it doesCan it change anything?

I built and measured two ways of giving the agent those facts. In the “tools” design the agent calls three read-only lookup tools (get_products, get_market_info, get_ordering_info). In the “inline” design the same facts are placed in the agent’s instructions, leaving suggest_contact as its only tool. Both answered all 8 graded test questions correctly, but the inline design needed fewer model calls and was cheaper and about twice as fast (Section 5), so it is what runs on the live site. The tools design stays in the code behind a setting, because it scales better if the product list grows large.

Model

The assistant uses gpt-5.6-luna through the course’s Portkey AI gateway (OpenAI Responses API format), the same setup used in earlier assignments. It is a small, inexpensive model: about $0.20 per million input tokens and $1.20 per million output tokens[2]. The task needs short, accurate answers from a small set of facts, not deep reasoning, so a small model is the right fit. Replies are capped at 350 output tokens per model call and about 80 words by instruction.

Walkthrough: one real run

A visitor on the Products page first asks “What flavors of cinnamon rolls do you have?”. The agent answers directly from the facts (prickly pear and mesquite bean, sold only at Goliad Market Days): one model call, no hand-off. They then ask “Can you ship a gift box of jelly to my aunt in Dallas? How much would it cost?”. The facts say deliveries are available on request and gift packaging is available, but there are no published prices or shipping costs. The agent says exactly that and calls suggest_contact; the code adds the hand-off button (Figure 6). Clicking it opens the Contact form with the topic set to Deliveries and a polite message already written (Figure 7). The visitor adds their name and email and sends it to Lisha.

Two caveats at 10,000 users. First, 30,000 questions a month is about 1,000 a day, above the site’s current daily cap of 400 questions, so the cap would need raising, deliberately. Second, Netlify’s free tier is now credit-based; third-party summaries disagree on exactly how many function calls it covers[3], so at that scale hosting might need a paid plan. For a market vendor, though, 100 users is the realistic case, and the AI costs about nine cents a month. The domain name is the only real fixed cost, typically $10–$20 per year.

What I did to keep cost down

  1. Small model, short answers. gpt-5.6-luna, a 350-token cap per call, and an ~80-word reply instruction.
  2. Fewer model calls. Putting the facts in the instructions (“inline”) instead of fetching them with tool calls cut the average from about 2 calls per question to 1.25–1.4.
  3. Answer cache. Opening questions answered earlier the same day (the three starter buttons send identical text) are served from storage. Measured: first ask 811 tokens and 1.8 s, second ask 0 tokens in 1 ms. The cache resets daily so the “next market day” is never stale.
  4. Gateway cache. The course’s Portkey gateway also returns stored responses for identical requests. In testing, identical requests were cache hits returned in about 0.1 s.
  5. Hard limits. At most 4 model calls per question, 20 questions per visitor per hour, and 400 questions or 400,000 tokens per day site-wide (Section 6). Even if every token were billed at the output rate, that caps spending at about $0.48 per day.

Did cost-cutting make the app worse?

I tested the main cost cut (inline facts instead of lookup tools) on 8 graded questions with automatic pass/fail checks: right flavors, correct next market date, gift boxes mentioned, cinnamon rolls not offered for delivery, no dollar amounts, a hand-off for prices and allergens, and “not published” for market hours. Result: no measurable quality loss. The inline design was 7–22% cheaper and roughly twice as fast in both runs. The savings vary between runs because the model doesn’t produce identical answers each time. Two honest limits: 8 questions is a small test set, and the inline design sends all the facts with every question, so it would get more expensive than tools if the product list grew to hundreds of items. The answer cache has a different trade-off: a cached answer can’t take earlier conversation into account, so it is only used for the first question in a conversation.

6. Safety

The risks that matter for a small public website with an AI assistant, and what I built against each. Every defense below was tested; results are from eval.mjs unless noted.

Attacks: prompt injection and misuse

Visitors can type anything, including instructions meant to hijack the assistant. Defenses, in layers:

  • Instructions: visitor messages are treated as questions, never as new rules, and the agent only discusses Rare Tastes of Texas.
  • Nothing worth stealing: the agent holds no secrets (the model key stays in the server function), has no write access, and its one tool only drafts a message the visitor must send.
  • Prompt-leak canary: a unique marker string sits in the instructions. If it, or the opening line of the instructions, ever appears in a reply, code replaces the reply with a refusal.
  • Provider filter: the model provider (Azure OpenAI, behind Portkey) blocks some jailbreak attempts outright. I first discovered this as a confusing “trouble right now” error; the code now turns it into a polite on-topic refusal.
  • Strict input checks: questions are capped at 400 characters, the conversation history at 6 turns, and history may only contain “user” and “assistant” roles, so a forged “system” message is rejected.

Agent malfunction: invented facts, wrong actions, loops

The most realistic harm is the assistant confidently making something up: a price, market hours, or worst of all an allergen fact. A customer with a child who has celiac disease must not be told “yes, it’s gluten free” by a guess. Defenses:

  • Facts only from the site, and an instruction to say “I don’t know” and hand off for prices, hours, ingredients, allergens, and shipping. In testing, it did this every time (price bait, ingredients and calories, “very hot?”, market opening time, and the celiac question).
  • Invented-price guard (code): Lisha publishes no prices online, so any dollar amount in a reply must be invented. Code replaces such a reply with “Prices aren’t listed online…” and a hand-off.
  • Loop guard: at most 4 model calls per question, then a fallback hand-off.
  • Length guard (900 characters) and a 20-second timeout.
  • No harmful actions possible: the only action drafts a message, and the visitor must read it and press Send.
  • A visible disclaimer under the chat box: “AI answers can be imperfect; Lisha always has the final word.”

To prove the code guards work even when the model misbehaves, the test script replaces the model with scripted bad replies:

Overspending

A bug or a bot hammering the assistant could run up the bill. Counters in Netlify Blobs enforce:

LimitValueTest

Whole site400 questions or 400,000 tokens per dayPASS: question 401 blocked; assistant tells visitors to use Contact Me

Together these put a ceiling of about $0.48 per day on the worst case, and the per-visitor limit stops a single abuser long before that.

Other risks covered

  • Contact form spam: a hidden “honeypot” field that bots fill in and people never see, plus Web3Forms’ own filtering.
  • Lost messages: if Web3Forms rejects a message, the form shows a “please try again” error rather than pretending to send.
  • Privacy: the site stores no visitor data. Visitor IPs are hashed for rate limiting, and the chat isn’t saved.
  • Leaked keys: the model key lives only in Netlify’s environment settings. A scan of the repository before the first commit confirmed neither key is in it.

8. Limits and next steps

What doesn’t work yet

  • Going live is still in progress. When this was written, Netlify was still issuing the security certificate for raretastesoftexas.com, and the site’s Netlify login protection needed to be switched off. The site must also be redeployed from GitHub for the assistant to run, since drag-and-drop deploys can’t host the server function.
  • Contact messages currently go to me, not to Lisha. The Web3Forms form was created under my account; its recipient must be changed to Lisha’s email address before launch.
  • The assistant runs on the course’s shared AI key. For long-term use it needs its own key and billing. The spending caps keep the cost tiny either way.
  • Missing facts: prices, market hours, and ingredient and allergen details aren’t published, so the assistant (correctly) hands those questions off. Adding them would let it answer more on its own.
  • Small test sets: 12 typical questions, 8 attacks, and 8 graded questions are evidence, not proof. Real visitors will ask things I didn’t think of.
  • Rate limits are approximate: counters aren’t perfectly atomic, so a burst could slightly exceed a limit, and the per-visitor limit can be dodged with many IP addresses. The site-wide daily cap is the backstop.
  • English only, and no analytics yet.

With another month

  1. Launch and pilot: finish the domain and recipient setup, deploy from GitHub, print QR-code cards for the market table, and collect the Section 7 numbers.
  2. Let Lisha update her own content: a simple editor or spreadsheet for flavors, “in stock this month,” and prices, so changes don’t need me. The assistant would pick them up automatically.
  3. “What’s available this month”: she sells seasonally, so a monthly availability list would answer the most common question directly.
  4. Spanish: many customers in South Texas would be better served in Spanish. The model can answer in Spanish with small changes to its instructions.
  5. Monitoring: a weekly email summary of questions asked, hand-offs, and spending, plus an alert if the daily cap is reached.
  6. A bigger evaluation set built from real visitor questions once the pilot is running.
